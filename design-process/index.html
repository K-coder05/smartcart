01 / THE PROBLEM

Dinner shouldn’t require
this much decision-making.

Students and young adults often want to cook more, but deciding what to make can become difficult when they also need to consider cost, dietary needs, time, and grocery planning.

$

Budget Constraints

Users want meals that fit realistic spending limits.

♡

Dietary Preferences

Vegetarian, vegan, allergy, and protein preferences affect recipe choices.

↻

Decision Fatigue

Choosing what to cook every day can become mentally exhausting.

⌑

Grocery Planning

Finding a recipe is only one part of the task—users still need to shop.

02 / RESEARCH

Understanding how people
decide what to cook.

01

Who are we designing for?

  • College students
  • Young adults living independently
  • People with limited grocery budgets
  • Users with dietary preferences or restrictions
  • Users who want fast meal-planning decisions
02

How we learned

User interviewsNeedfindingObservationCompetitive analysisPrototype testing
Key insights

What we heard changed what we built.

01

Users want fewer choices, not more choices.

Too many recipe options can create additional decision fatigue.

02

Budget matters at the moment of choosing.

Users want cost information directly connected to recipe recommendations.

03

Filters need to reflect real dietary behavior.

Users asked for vegetarian, vegan, allergy, high protein, and no red meat options.

07 / USER TESTING

Watching users showed us
what the prototype missed.

Participants
2+
Tasks
Recipe Search · Recipe Details · Saving Recipes
Format
Moderated usability testing

✓ What worked

  • Filters were easy to understand.
  • Users successfully navigated recipe matches.
  • Users understood that the heart saves a recipe.
  • Recipe ingredient information was useful.

! What needed improvement

  • Login failures did not show enough feedback.
  • Users wanted password recovery options.
  • Instructions needed more detailed quantities.
  • Prices should reflect real grocery-store costs.
  • One participant requested “No red meat.”

IN THEIR WORDS

“I feel like there should be a no red meat option.”Participant 1
“I would want more detailed instructions, especially how much of each ingredient to use.”Participant 2
“The filters were easy to use.”Participant 1

08 / ITERATIONS

We turned feedback into
specific design changes.

01

Login feedback

BEFORE

Login could fail without clearly explaining why.

→
AFTER

Add clear error messages and password recovery.

02

Allergy filters

BEFORE

Users could only choose allergies from a preset list.

→
AFTER

Users can now type and add their own specific allergens.

03

Budget input

BEFORE

A slider made it difficult to select an exact budget amount.

→
AFTER

Users can now enter a precise budget amount directly.

04

Price information

BEFORE

Users could only set a budget based on price per serving.

→
AFTER

Users can now choose between per-serving price and total store price.

10 / WHAT WE LEARNED

Good usability often comes
from the small details.

↗

Visible Feedback

Users should always understand whether an action succeeded or failed.

◎

Realistic Information

Price information is more valuable when it reflects real-world grocery costs.

♡

Design for Real Behavior

Filters and workflows should reflect how users actually describe their food preferences.
